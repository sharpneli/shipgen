# Superstructure contents & size, 1890–1970

*Researched 2026-10-03; §6 (heights) added 2026-10-04. Companion to `crew-space-model.md` (§7 Placement) and `hull-weight-model.md` (A_super, `superstructure_t_per_m2`).*

> **Data honesty.** Rows marked **(S)** are sourced (see §7). Rows marked **(E)** are my estimates from general naval-architecture knowledge, plans and the sourced anchors. Treat (E) numbers as tuning defaults, not facts. The firmest hard numbers are the 1970s frigate volume split (Knox, FFG-7); the pre-1940 shares are the weakest.

---

## TL;DR

- **For most of 1890–1945, people lived in the hull, not the superstructure.** The superstructure held ship control (bridge, chart house, conning tower), fire control, searchlights, boats and a handful of sea cabins. Crew messdecks and most officers' cabins were on the main, upper and lower decks inside the hull.
- **The superstructure grew in three waves, for different reasons:**
  1. **1905–1920, fire control.** Long-range gunnery needed height. Tripods, director towers and compass platforms grew. Post-Jutland, bridges got bigger and more crowded. Still almost no accommodation.
  2. **1925–1945, command, then radar and AA.** Tower bridges (Nelson's "Queen Anne's Mansions", Japanese pagodas, KGV, Iowa) added sheltered working space and flag/captain sea quarters. WWII then piled on radar, CIC, AA directors and light guns, so ships became topweight-limited.
  3. **1950–1970, volume and electronics.** Electronics, CIC, air conditioning, better habitability and helicopters made ships *volume-limited* rather than weight-limited. Aluminium deckhouses made it affordable in topweight. Superstructures went full-width and long, and accommodation started moving up into them.
- **Size, as share of total enclosed volume (rough):** about 5 % on an 1890s battleship → about 10 % on WWI/interwar capital ships → about 12–18 % on WWII destroyers → **28–29 % on Knox (1969) and FFG-7 (1977) (S)**.
- **Accommodation in the superstructure (rough):** about 0–5 % of living space before 1940, about 5–15 % in WWII, about 25–40 % on late-1960s escorts (E).
- **Heights (§6):** the navigating bridge sits about 1.3–2× the highest turret's gun height above the waterline, and the fire-control top 2–3×. The ratio holds from destroyers (top ~16 m) to Yamato (~40 m).
- **The countervailing pressures** were topweight/stability, blast and splinter damage (light plating), wave damage to aluminium, and in the 1950s nuclear blast (RN Type 15 kept a deliberately low superstructure).

## 1. Terms

Same split as the Watson–Gilfillan E-numeral in `hull-weight-model.md`:

- **Full-width erections** (fo'c'sle, poop, raised quarterdeck) extend the hull to the sides. They behave like hull volume: good for messdecks and stores, and they add freeboard.
- **Deckhouses** sit inboard of the sides on the strength deck. Pre-1950 they were narrow (about 50 % of beam on a Fletcher, about 80 % at the forward end) (S). By the 1960s escort deckhouses were near full-width.
- **Towers / masts** (tripod, pagoda, tower bridge, lattice) hold height-critical items with little usable floor area.

In this doc "superstructure" means everything above the strength deck except the hull's own fo'c'sle. Where a full-length fo'c'sle deck exists (e.g. Type 15 conversions, many 1950s escorts), it is counted as hull.

## 2. Era by era

### 2.1 Pre-dreadnought, 1890–1905

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Armoured conning tower with a light chart house/pilot house over it, a flying bridge or "flying deck" carrying boats on skid beams, funnels and ventilators, searchlight platforms, fighting tops on military masts, and on many ships a casemate or battery deck for the secondary guns. |
| **Accommodation** | In the hull. Admiral's and captain's cabins and the galley were typically on the main deck aft (USS Olympia 1895: captain's lodgings, admiral's cabin and stateroom and galley on the main deck; officers' country on the berth deck; sick bay forward; pilot house and bridge on top, conning tower under the forward bridge) (S). Crew slung hammocks on the gun/main/berth decks over the mess tables. RN tradition put officers aft. |
| **Size (E)** | ~4–7 % of enclosed volume. The tall hull sides (and French tumblehome hulls) held most functions, so the superstructure stayed light. |
| **Drivers** | Command needed only short sight lines: battle ranges were a few thousand metres. Boats had to be stowed somewhere above deck. |

### 2.2 Dreadnought & WWI, 1905–1920

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Tripod foremast with spotting top and later director; compass platform and admiral's bridge; conning tower(s); searchlight towers; boat deck between funnels. Revenge class: eight searchlights, four of them on the bridge; secondary directors fitted on either side of the compass platform from March 1917 (S). |
| **Accommodation** | Still in the hull. **HMS Dreadnought (1906) was the first battleship with most officers' quarters forward**, near the bridge. It was disliked: cabins small and scattered among machinery, cables and vibration, with the men's messdecks aft and inconvenient (S). The RN reverted to officers aft on later ships (E; widely reported, not verified here). |
| **Size (E)** | ~6–9 %. Growth was mostly *height*, not floor area. |
| **Drivers** | Gunnery ranges went from ~5 km to 15+ km, needing tall director positions. Jutland pushed enlarged bridges, more directors and more searchlights. Complements grew too: Revenge class from ~909–940 in 1917 to 1,012–1,240 by the early 1920s (S), still berthed in the hull. |

### 2.3 Interwar tower bridges, 1920–1936

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Enclosed multi-level towers: navigating bridge, admiral's (flag) bridge, plotting and signal offices, AA and main directors on top, sea cabins. Nelson/Rodney's octagonal tower gave "spacious, weatherproof working spaces for the navigating officers and any flag officers embarked", only splinter-armoured except the conning tower at its base (S). The Japanese pagoda rebuilds stacked searchlight, lookout and control platforms on strengthened tripods to 40+ m above the waterline (S). |
| **Accommodation** | Mostly hull. The superstructure now usually held the captain's and admiral's **sea cabins** and some staff offices. In-port cabins stayed in the hull. |
| **Size (E)** | ~8–12 % on capital ships. Cruisers were similar; destroyers ~8–12 % (a bridge block plus small after deckhouses). |
| **Drivers** | Command and control became an office job. Treaty weight limits rewarded light, high structures over heavy armoured conning towers. Nelson used aluminium fittings to save weight (S). |

### 2.4 WWII, 1936–1945

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Bridge block with radar office and, from 1942–43, the **CIC** (USN "radar plot" became CIC in winter 1942–43 (S)); radio rooms; AA directors; radar masts; 40 mm and 20 mm tubs with ready-use lockers. |
| **Accommodation** | Battleships: still mostly hull. USS Iowa had enlisted berthing on the main, 2nd and 3rd decks (racks 3–5 high), the wardroom and senior officers on the main deck, and **the captain's in-port cabin and junior officer staterooms on the 01 level**, with sea cabins on the 04 and 08 levels (S). Destroyers: crew in the fo'c'sle and aft hull; officers forward in the hull or deckhouse (E). |
| **Size (E)** | Destroyers ~12–18 % (Fletcher deckhouse ~50 % of beam, ~80 % forward (S)). Battleships ~8–12 %. |
| **Drivers / counter-drivers** | Wartime additions made ships **topweight-critical**. Fletchers lost the forward torpedo bank in the 1945 anti-kamikaze refit to make room and weight for an elevated director platform and more 40 mm (S). Battle-class destroyers traded the midships 4.5 in for STAAG mounts (S). Complements rose 20–80 % without new berths: men slept where they could (see `crew-space-model.md` §6, `crowd`). |

### 2.5 Early Cold War, 1945–1958

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Larger radar and ECM offices, a bigger CIC, sonar control, air-conditioning plant, NBC citadel boundaries. |
| **Accommodation** | Begins moving up. Forrest Sherman (1955): **aluminium superstructure**, air-conditioned spaces, bunk lights, and **enlisted messdecks moved to the main deck next to the galleys** (S). |
| **Size (E)** | ~15–22 %. |
| **Counter-drivers** | Aluminium deckhouses were weak in a seaway: Barry's deckhouse stringers distorted ("taffy pull") and expansion joints were mandated class-wide; Manley's radar and comms spaces were stove in by a wave in 1957, killing two (S). The RN Type 15 deliberately kept a **low superstructure against nuclear blast**, gaining volume instead by extending the fo'c'sle aft, and was designed to fight sealed against CBRN effects (S). |

### 2.6 Volume-limited escorts, 1958–1970

| | |
|---|---|
| **Typical contents** | Long, near full-width deckhouses carrying CIC/ops room, electronics, offices, helicopter hangar, a share of the accommodation, and large radar masts (Battle-class pickets needed a mast straddling the full beam for the 4-ton Type 965 aerial (S)). |
| **Accommodation (E)** | ~25–40 % of living space on new escorts, mainly officers' and CPO cabins and some messes. Ratings' messes were still mostly in the hull. |
| **Size (S)** | **Knox (FF-1052, 1969): superstructure 141,160 ft³ of 503,403 ft³ = 28 %.** **FFG-7 (1977): 153,390 ft³ of 531,980 ft³ = 29 %.** Personnel volume was about 21 % (Knox) and 20 % (FFG-7) of the whole ship. Garzke & Kerr: "a clear trend… toward larger superstructure volumes due to the demands for command and control space volume, suitable places to mount weapons and sensors, and space to maintain helicopters." Spruance (1975): aluminium superstructure welded to the hull via bimetallic strips, with space and power reserved for growth (S). |
| **Drivers** | Habitability: volume per man went from **210 ft³ (5.9 m³) on DD-692 (1944)** to **695 ft³ (19.7 m³) on FFG-7** (S). Weapons and electronics took 15–20 % of total volume on both US and Soviet ships (S). |

## 3. Trend summary (for curves)

| Year | Superstructure share of enclosed volume | Share of living space in superstructure | What dominated the superstructure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1890 | 5 % (E) | 0–3 % (E) | Conning, boats, searchlights |
| 1910 | 7 % (E) | 0–3 % (E) | Fire-control height |
| 1930 | 10 % (E) | 3–8 % (E) | Command spaces, sea cabins |
| 1944 | 12–18 % DD, 8–12 % BB (E) | 5–15 % (E) | Radar, CIC, AA |
| 1955 | 15–22 % (E) | 10–25 % (E) | Electronics, habitability; aluminium |
| 1969 | **28 % (S, Knox)** | 25–40 % (E) | Ops room, electronics, hangar, cabins |

Capital ships stayed lower than escorts throughout, because their deep hulls had volume to spare. Small escorts ran out of hull volume first.

## 4. Why things moved up: a causal checklist

1. **Height-critical items** (directors, radar, lookouts, searchlights) must be high, so they always define a minimum superstructure.
2. **The hull fills first** with machinery, magazines, fuel, double bottom and torpedo protection, in that order of priority. Whatever volume is left over in the hull goes to people; the overflow goes up.
3. **Volume per man roughly tripled** between 1944 and 1977 (S), while hulls didn't grow proportionally, so living space overflowed into the superstructure.
4. **Electronics and CIC** are low-density volume: they want to be near the bridge and the antennas, which puts them in the superstructure.
5. **Light structure enables it**: aluminium deckhouses (Forrest Sherman, Spruance, FFG-7) cut topweight per m³ at the cost of fire and wave vulnerability.

**Pressures pushing the other way:** stability margins, splinter and blast vulnerability, nuclear blast (1950s), radar/visual signature (late-war submarines cut their superstructure about 50 % (S)), wind area (Nelson ran aground off Southsea in 1934 partly through windage on its tower (S)).

## 5. Game hooks (suggested, not calibrated)

- **Placement rule for `crew-space-model.md` V_live:** allocate to hull spaces first (fore and aft of the machinery/magazine block). Overflow goes to the superstructure. Superstructure share then emerges from the design rather than being scripted per era. Use §3 as a validation band.
- **Fixed superstructure "core" by tech era:** conning/bridge, directors, radar/CIC volumes, each year-gated. Accommodation fills around it.
- **Weight:** feed `superstructure_t_per_m2` with a material switch (steel ≈ 1.0, aluminium ≈ 0.55–0.65 of steel weight (E)). Aluminium should raise fire-load and wave-damage risk (Manley, Belknap 1975 collision fire).
- **Damage:** superstructure hits kill off-watch men only where accommodation actually sits there. Pre-1940 ships mostly lose command, fire control and AA crews instead. Post-1960 ships lose sleeping crews and electronics together.
- **Stability:** every m³ added above the strength deck raises KG. WWII refits that add AA should force trades (torpedo tubes, a gun, boats), as historically.

## 6. Heights: bridge and fire-control top vs main turrets

Heights are above the waterline, in metres. Only a few were findable online (**S**). The rest (**E**) are estimates: published freeboard + typical turret geometry (gun axis ≈ deck at that station + 1–1.5 m exposed barbette + 1.5–2.5 m trunnion height), checked against remembered general-arrangement drawings. Scaled profile drawings could not be fetched to measure; doing that is the next step.

| Ship (size) | Lowest turret guns | Superfiring turret guns | Navigating bridge | Fire-control top | Top ÷ superfiring turret |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| USS Alabama 1898 (11,500 t pre-dreadnought) | ~8.5 E (fo'c'sle deck 6.1 m forward, 4 m aft, **S**) | none | ~11–13 E | fighting top ~20–24 E | ~2.5× the lowest turret |
| HMS Dreadnought 1906 (18,000 t) | ~10 E | none (all turrets at one level) | ~16 E | spotting top ~28–30 E | ~3× the lowest turret |
| Fletcher-class destroyer 1942 (2,900 t) | ~7 E | ~9.5 E | ~12–13 E | Mk 37 director ~16–17 E | ~1.7× |
| Cleveland-class light cruiser 1942 (14,000 t) | ~9.5 E | ~12.5 E | ~17–18 E | gun director ~22–24 E | ~1.8× |
| Bismarck 1940 (50,000 t) | ~11 E | ~14.5 E | ~20 E | foretop ~31 **S** | ~2.1× |
| Iowa 1943 (57,000 t) | ~11.5 E | ~16 E | ~20–22 E | forward Mk 38 director 30–38 **S** (sources give "about 100 ft" and 126 ft) | ~2.2× |
| Yamato 1941 (72,000 t) | ~13 E | ~17 E | ~28–30 E | 15 m rangefinder ~39–40 (**S**: 100 ft above weather deck; freeboard 10 m fwd / 6.4 m aft) | ~2.3× |

Supporting facts: 5 in destroyer mounts carry the gun axis 1.37–1.47 m above their deck (**S**, NavWeaps); Bismarck's freeboard amidships was 4.45–5.67 m with a 15 m hull depth (**S**).

### 6.1 What sets each height

- **Turrets are set by freeboard.** The lowest turret sits on the fo'c'sle, so its guns are at about freeboard + 3 m. Each superfiring level adds 3–4.5 m so its guns can fire over the turret ahead. Turret height therefore scales with hull depth: ~7 m on a destroyer, ~13 m on Yamato.
- **The bridge has to see over the superfiring turret.** In practice it sits 3–6 m above that turret's roof on small ships and 5–13 m on battleships. 1930s tower bridges (Yamato, Nelson) went highest.
- **The fire-control top is set by range.** Horizon distance d_km ≈ 3.57·√h_m: a 12 m turret sees ~12 km, Bismarck's 31 m foretop ~20 km, Yamato's 40 m rangefinder ~22.5 km. That roughly matches each era's expected battle range, which is why tops grew from ~20 m fighting tops in the 1890s to 30–40 m by WWII.
- **Caps:** topweight (Western critics called the pagoda masts "Christmas trees"; Fubuki rebuilds cut the bridge and funnels after the Fourth Fleet Incident, **S**) and practical limits. US masts had to clear the Brooklyn Bridge to reach the New York Navy Yard (**S**).
- **Masts and radar go higher still,** but they are unmanned: battleship mast tops and radar ~45–55 m, destroyer masts ~25–30 m (E).

### 6.2 Game defaults (suggested)

```
h_gun[i]   = freeboard_at(x_i) + 1.2 + trunnion(cal)        # trunnion ≈ 1.4 m (5 in) … 2.5 m (16–18 in), E
h_gun_sf   = h_gun_below + 3.0 … 4.5                        # superfiring step
h_bridge   = max(h_gun) + 4…6   (+8…12 for a tower bridge)
h_fc_top   = clamp(2.0 … 2.5 × max(h_gun), ≤ stability limit)
range_vis  = 3.57 * sqrt(h_fc_top)  km                      # optical horizon (target height adds 3.57·√h_target)
```

Every metre of height adds topweight (KG), windage and target silhouette, so height should be a player trade-off against stability, not a fixed era value.
